RILEY: Welcome back to Finance Fundamentals! I'm Riley with Alex, and today we're tackling debt—something that, let's be honest, most of us deal with. Alex, should I be freaking out about credit card debt?

ALEX: Riley, debt isn't inherently bad, but high-interest debt like credit cards needs serious attention. If you're paying 18-24% interest, that debt is actively working against your financial future.

RILEY: What makes credit card debt so dangerous?

ALEX: Compound interest working against you! For example, if you have ₹50,000 at 20% interest and just stick to minimum payments, it could take decades to pay off and cost you more than double the original amount.

RILEY: More than double? That’s wild.

ALEX: That's the power of compound interest! When it works for you—like in investments—it’s amazing. But in debt, it can be financially devastating.

RILEY: So what's the strategy? Pay off debt before investing?

ALEX: Typically, yes, for high-interest debt like credit cards or instant loans. If you're paying 20% interest, you'd need to earn even more on investments just to break even—and that's not likely. Clearing these debts should be a priority.

RILEY: What about lower-interest debts, like education or home loans?

ALEX: That's where it gets interesting! If your education loan is at 7% and you can invest in mutual funds or stocks that have historically returned 10-12% per year, it might make sense to pay just the minimum on the loan while investing the rest. It comes down to comparing your interest rates and long-term returns.

RILEY: So it depends on the interest rate?

ALEX: Exactly. High-interest debt—clear it as fast as possible. Lower-interest loans—be strategic.

RILEY: What about different debt payoff strategies? I’ve heard about snowball and avalanche...

ALEX: Snowball means you pay off the smallest balances first for quick wins, while avalanche means you pay off the highest interest rate loans first to save the most money mathematically.

RILEY: Which is better?

ALEX: Avalanche is optimal for saving money, but snowball can be more motivating—knocking out one entire loan feels great! Personal finance is about what works for you. If seeing progress keeps you motivated, snowball might be for you.

RILEY: How do I choose between the two?

ALEX: Be honest with yourself. Are you motivated by quick wins, or do you want to pay the least amount possible? Pick the strategy you can stick with for the long haul.

RILEY: Let's talk about credit scores—how do they actually work?

ALEX: Credit scores are basically a report card on your borrowing habits. In India, your CIBIL score or Experian score reflects how reliably you pay off debt. Scores above 750 are generally considered good.

RILEY: What factors affect my score?

ALEX: Payment history is about 35%—always pay on time. Credit utilisation—how much of your available credit you actually use—counts for 30%. The length of your credit history, different types of credit, and the number of recent applications make up the rest.

RILEY: What’s a good utilisation rate?

ALEX: Try to stay under 30% of your credit limit—ideally under 10%. For example, if your total card limit is ₹1,00,000, keep the outstanding amount below ₹30,000 for a healthy score.

RILEY: I pay my card off every month—does that matter?

ALEX: It depends when the bank reports your balance to credit bureaus. Sometimes, even if you pay in full each month, a high statement balance can briefly impact your score. Consider paying down your card before the statement date or making more frequent payments.

RILEY: What about the length of my credit history?

ALEX: Don't close your oldest cards, even if you don’t use them regularly. The longer your credit history, the better your score. Maybe make a small payment once a year to keep the card active.

RILEY: And new credit inquiries?

ALEX: Every time you apply for a new loan or card, it counts as a ‘hard inquiry’ and can slightly lower your score temporarily. Don’t worry if you’re rate-shopping for things like home loans—try to do it within a short timeframe.

RILEY: How often should I check my score?

ALEX: At least once or twice a year, and always before making a major loan application. You can check your credit report for free from bureaus like CIBIL or Equifax once a year.

RILEY: What if I find errors in my report?

ALEX: Raise a dispute with the credit bureau and your bank immediately! Mistakes happen, and fixing them can significantly improve your score.

RILEY: How long does it take to improve a credit score?

ALEX: It varies. Paying down outstanding balances can show results in 1-2 months. If you’ve missed payments, it can take 6-12 months of responsible behaviour to rebuild your score.

RILEY: So it’s not permanent damage?

ALEX: Not at all! Your score is constantly updating. Good financial habits eventually override past mistakes.

RILEY: Any myths about credit scores you want to bust?

ALEX: Absolutely! Checking your own score doesn’t hurt it. Carrying a small ongoing balance doesn’t help—paying in full is best. And closing old cards can lower your score, so only do it if necessary.

RILEY: So the goal is responsible usage, not avoidance?

ALEX: Exactly! Used well, credit builds your financial reputation and gives you access to better loan rates and financial opportunities.

RILEY: Any final debt and credit advice?

ALEX: Automate at least your minimum monthly payments to avoid late fees. Choose whichever debt strategy keeps you motivated. And remember—your financial journey is a marathon, not a sprint.

RILEY: That’s awesome. Next episode, let’s talk about the basics of investing! Thanks, Alex!

ALEX: Thanks, Riley! Remember, debt isn’t a moral failing. It’s a practical, and totally solvable, financial challenge—just tackle it step by step.
